# WIN PLAYBOOK

This document is not a promise of first place. It is the competitive strategy for giving RIPPLE BD the strongest credible submission we can produce.

## 1. The winning position we want judges to see

Not:
> “We have an interesting awareness idea.”

But:
> “We found a real problem, used evidence-backed behavioral mechanics, built a usable prototype, tested it locally, measured learning, improved the product from feedback, and have a realistic plan to scale.”

That is the entire strategy.

---

## 2. Six competitive pillars

### Pillar 1 — Relevance
Show one clear Bangladesh/youth problem.

Use:
- credible sources,
- one or two strong data points,
- local conversations/pilot insight.

Avoid:
- a slide full of generic global statistics.

### Pillar 2 — Demonstrable innovation
Our innovation is the **closed learning loop**:

**natural decision → visible ripple → human consequence → rewind → safer retry → SHIELD response → retention measurement**

Do not claim simulation/prebunking itself is new.

### Pillar 3 — Feasibility
Judges should believe:
“this team can actually deliver this with the fellowship.”

Proof:
- working browser MVP,
- zero-paid stack,
- no backend dependency,
- small scoped scenarios,
- dated implementation plan.

### Pillar 4 — Evidence
Best proof before deadline:
- 20–30 person local pilot,
- baseline vs post,
- completion rate,
- one usability insight,
- one product change caused by user feedback.

A small honest pilot beats a huge invented reach claim.

### Pillar 5 — Community rootedness
Demonstrate that the product was shaped by people from the target division.

Strong evidence:
- youth co-design conversations,
- pilot participants,
- a confirmed venue/community host if possible,
- local language and realistic behavior.

### Pillar 6 — Team
A 3-person team should look deliberately complementary:
- build,
- evidence/safety,
- community/story.

Every member should have a clear ownership sentence.

## 3. The 60-second judge moment

We want a judge to *feel* the idea before hearing the full explanation.

### Demo
1. Viral fictional post appears.
2. Judge chooses SHARE or VERIFY.
3. Ripple unfolds.
4. A human/community consequence appears.
5. Interface freezes.
6. **REWIND.**
7. Judge tries again with safer tools.
8. Presenter explains the product thesis.

### Why it matters
Most applications are described.
RIPPLE should be **experienced**.

---

## 4. The evidence hierarchy

Use evidence in this order:

1. **Our working prototype**
2. **Our local pilot**
3. **Our local co-design/user feedback**
4. **Bangladesh-relevant credible data**
5. **Global peer-reviewed/proven intervention evidence**
6. Aspirational future claims

The farther down the list, the less it should dominate the pitch.

## 6. Product quality gates

### Gate A — 30-second clarity
Can a new user explain RIPPLE after 30–60 seconds?

### Gate B — No-explanation usability
Can a pilot user finish without a developer sitting beside them?

### Gate C — Measurable learning
Does the product create a score that is transparent and defensible?

### Gate D — Safety
Can any scenario be reasonably mistaken as an accusation against a real person/group? If yes, rewrite it.

### Gate E — Demo reliability
Can the core experience run with no API/backend failure risk?

## 8. Interview defense

Every teammate should be able to answer:
- What problem are we solving?
- Why this method?
- What is original?
- What is borrowed from research?
- What did our pilot show?
- What did we change after the pilot?
- What happens if pilot results are weak?
- Why is the product safe?
- Why is this feasible on the available grant?
- What exactly exists by February 2027?

No teammate should answer “the tech person knows that” for a core question.

---

## 9. If pilot results are weak

Do not hide them.

Example:
> “Our first pilot showed users loved REWIND but misunderstood the score. We redesigned the result screen and retested it.”

That can be stronger than suspiciously perfect numbers because it demonstrates iteration.
